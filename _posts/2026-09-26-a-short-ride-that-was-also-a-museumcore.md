I came to know about Zapurza, an art and cultural museum, about a year ago and had been meaning to visit it. So when I thought about where I could ride this weekend, I looked it up on Google Maps. It showed that it was about 35 km from my place. I thought, why not turn this into a quick riding circuit?

Zapurza is located in Kudje village, on the banks of the Khadakwasla backwaters. From my place, it is about 35-odd km, but instead of simply riding there and coming back the same way, I thought I could take a longer route. I could first head towards Pirangut, then towards Temghar and Lavasa and then on the way back, instead of going to Pirangut, I could take the road that breaks off towards Khadakwasla dam from the "other side". When I say "other side", I mean the side that does not have Sinhagad on it. I had never really been to this side of Khadakwasla. Typically, whenever I have gone towards Sinhagad or Khadakwasla, I have taken the road that breaks right off the Pune-Bengaluru highway at Warje and leads towards Donje. That's the route I have usually taken. But Zapurza is at Kudje village, on the other side of the dam. This was the circuit I had planned the night before, and it showed that the total distance would be close to 130-140 km, home to home.

The next morning, I set off a little late because I had been catching up on some sleep that had been missing over the past few days. I ditched my usual morning tea at Yewale Amrutulya and headed straight towards Hinjewadi.

From there, I took the road towards Pirangut and, in no time, I was away from the city and into the countryside. This time, I did not have any music playing while I rode. It was just empty roads, my bike, my thoughts and the songs I was occasionally humming to myself.

The road until Pirangut was okay. I did not even realise when I reached Pirangut. However, this was a familiar road. I had been to Lavasa once through this route. It was after Pirangut, when the road towards Temghar Dam started, that things became really scenic. I stopped to click a few photos and, after riding for a few kilometres, spotted a very small restaurant. I was instantly reminded of the time when I had been to Lavasa with my friends. This was exactly where we had stopped for tea on our way back. It brought back some fond memories of happy times.

The restaurant had just opened for the day, so there wasn't much on offer. Everything was made to order. I decided to order some potato bhaji and tea. While it was being prepared, I took the opportunity to click a few pictures from a spot overlooking the lush green valley. I think I spent close to 30 minutes there before realising that Lavasa was still another 30 km or so away.

When I started off from there, I was contemplating whether I should actually continue towards Temghar and then Lavasa, or not go that far and take a road towards Zapurza instead. My thought was that if I went all the way to Lavasa, it would take longer to reach the museum and I wouldn't be able to spend as much time there. I kept contemplating and finally decided that it was better to skip Temghar and Lavasa for this ride. I could always go there another day and spend enough time at the museum.

That turned out to be the right call.

I modified the Google Maps route, which suggested that instead of going towards Temghar, I could take a turn into a village called Mutha. Yes, there is actually a village named Mutha. I am not really sure whether the village is named after the Mutha River, the river is named after the village, or both are named after something else. But Mutha village was very quaint, and there was something really, really beautiful about it.

I also noticed that the road through Mutha runs alongside the Pune Ring Road construction. For throughout the road that goes through Mutha village, I was accompanied by the construction activity. In no time, I was on the road running alongside the Khadakwasla backwaters, heading towards Kudje village, where Zapurza is located.

The Zapurza campus is spread across seven acres and has 10 galleries or display spaces, along with workshop areas, an auditorium and an amphitheatre. The entrance fee is ₹250, and there are additional workshops and activities that you can opt for and pay for separately. Zapurza is an initiative of Ajit Gadgil of P. N. Gadgil & Sons. Much of the collection comes from the broader collection associated with the initiative, and the museum has paintings, sculptures, antiques, textiles, jewellery, lamps, traditional art and, interestingly, lithographs and oleographs from the Raja Ravi Varma Press.

The museum begins with artwork and material related to Raja Ravi Varma. I knew about Raja Ravi Varma and that he was a great artist. What I did not know was that he had set up a lithographic printing press in Ghatkopar, Mumbai, in 1894, which was later moved to Malavli near Lonavala in 1899 - yes, in our very own Pune.  The press played a huge role in making his artwork available to a much wider audience.

<img src="/assets/images/Zapurza/Raja-Ravi-Verma.jpeg" style="width: 100%;border:solid 1px #ccc; border-radius:2px;">

What fascinated me even more was the printing technique - Oleography. It was a form of colour lithography used to reproduce paintings. The process was incredibly labour-intensive. An image would be prepared on a  stone, and the stone would be treated using gum arabic and a  nitric acid solution. Different stones could then be used for different colours, with the paper passing through the press multiple times to build up the final image.

<img src="/assets/images/Zapurza/Oleography-stones.jpeg" style="width: 100%;border:solid 1px #ccc; border-radius:2px;">

So essentially, one painting had to be broken down into its different colours and tones and then diligently reconstructed through multiple printing passes. There was an example of this technique on display at the museum, and it was fascinating to see just how much effort went into creating what, to us today, looks like a single printed picture. This technique was a big thing in the late 18th and early 19th century but is now extinct.

I was also unaware of the huge influence Raja Ravi Varma had on how Indians came to visualise Hindu gods and mythological characters. Many of the familiar representations of divine figures that we see even today can be traced back to his work and its reproductions. He is widely regarded as the "Father of Modern Indian Art".

Another gallery that I absolutely loved was the one dedicated to the history of Indian cinema. I did not know that Prabhat Film Company, one of the pioneering film studios of early Indian cinema, was founded in Kolhapur in 1929 and later moved to Pune in 1933. The place where we have FTII today is where Prabhat Studios used to be. Fascinating.

<img src="/assets/images/Zapurza/Prabhat-Studios.jpeg" style="width: 100%;border:solid 1px #ccc; border-radius:2px;">

It was in the same gallery that I saw a gramophone and a radio from the 1930s. The person acting as a guide for the gallery was kind enough to tell me that the gramophone was actually functional, and he even played a song for us. I had never seen a gramophone in action until that moment.

<img src="/assets/images/Zapurza/Gramophone.jpg" style="width: 100%;border:solid 1px #ccc; border-radius:2px;">

I marvelled at how it worked and, at the same time, absolutely loved watching it. There were several other galleries featuring different kinds of lamps. There was one dedicated to Bengal's culture. Another displayed silver and gold ornaments, some of which date back around two centuries. Zapurza's collection also includes textiles, a brief section about the beginnings of P.N Gadgil's business and their journey and other traditional Indian crafts.

There was also an entire gallery dedicated to Sant Balu Mama, the revered shepherd-saint of Maharashtra. I had heard of Balu Mama before, but I knew very little about him. What fascinated me was learning about his association with the **Deccani** sheep and the tradition of shepherding around his legacy. His story is closely connected with the preservation of the flock and the cultural importance of sheep and wool in Maharastra. There is a foundation named Balu Mama vikas foundation in Admapur (dist. Kolhapur) that works for preservation of his legacy and welfare of shepherds.

The museum campus also has an amphitheatre overlooking the Khadakwasla backwaters. What a scenic backdrop! Just next to the amphitheatre is a cafe. I was really hungry by then and it was also lunchtime, but I had to get back home for lunch. So I just grabbed a quick snack, satiated myself and headed back home.

<img src="/assets/images/Zapurza/Amphitheatre.jpeg" style="width: 100%;border:solid 1px #ccc; border-radius:2px;">

I couldn't do the pottery workshop because I had already spent close to a couple of hours at the museum and needed to get back home sooner. I am certainly planning to come back here with Kittu next time. I think she'll love the pottery workshop. It was roughly a 1.5-hour ride back from there, and I was home by around 3 PM.

This was a great ride, but it was more about museums, art and culture than scenic spots and the thrill of riding. I don't consider myself a very artsy person, but I still loved the museum and, more than anything else, I loved discovering these new things about my own city and the places around it.

If you love art, history and culture, Zapurza is definitely worth a visit.
